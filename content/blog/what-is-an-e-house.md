**An e-house (electrical house, power house or power distribution centre) is a prefabricated, transportable building that arrives on site with its electrical equipment already installed, wired and tested: switchgear, motor control centres, drives, transformers, UPS, lighting, HVAC and fire detection.** Instead of building an electrical room on site and installing gear piece by piece, you set the e-house on its foundation, connect the incoming and outgoing cables and commission it.

E-houses are used wherever site labour is expensive, scarce or slow: mines, oil and gas facilities, remote northern sites, renewable projects, water plants and, increasingly, data centres.

## What goes inside

A typical e-house can include:

- **Medium-voltage switchgear**, either metal-clad or load-interrupter
- **Low-voltage switchgear or switchboards** and **motor control centres**
- **Variable-frequency drives** and harmonic filters
- **Dry-type transformers** for auxiliary and lighting power
- **UPS and battery systems**, DC panels and control power
- **Protection and control panels**, SCADA and communications
- **Building systems**: HVAC, heaters, lighting, fire detection and suppression, emergency lighting, grounding grid connections and cable entries

Large oil-filled transformers usually sit outside the e-house on their own pads, cabled or bus-ducted in.

## E-house vs site-built electrical room

| | E-house | Site-built room |
|---|---|---|
| Where the work happens | In the factory, in parallel with site civil works | On site, after the building is ready |
| Schedule | Shorter, because building and equipment progress at the same time | Longer, in sequence |
| Testing | Full factory acceptance test (FAT) of the integrated system | Mostly tested after installation |
| Site labour | Much less: set, connect, commission | Large electrical crew for longer |
| Quality control | Factory conditions | Weather and site conditions |
| Relocation | Can be moved and reused | Fixed |
| Limits | Shipping size and weight; foundations ready on arrival | Fewer size limits |

The schedule benefit is the main reason. Civil works and the e-house can progress at the same time, and testing in the factory catches wiring and integration problems before the house ships.

## Decisions to make early

### 1. Size, splits and shipping

Road transport limits width, height and weight, and those limits vary by province and route. Large e-houses are built as several **modules** that are bolted and sealed together on site, with bus and cable splits planned at the module joints. Check the route early, especially for remote sites served by **winter roads** or summer **sealift**.

### 2. Heat load and HVAC

Drives, transformers and UPS systems put a lot of heat into a closed room. Size the HVAC for the worst summer day *and* the coldest night. Northern sites need heaters sized for start-up from cold, not just for steady running.

### 3. Arc flash and venting

If the switchgear is **arc-resistant**, the house needs a plenum or duct to vent arc gases safely outside. That affects the roof, walls and layout, so decide it before the structure is designed. Our guide to [arc flash and CSA Z462](/blog/arc-flash-csa-z462) explains the accessibility types.

### 4. Cable entry and foundations

Decide on bottom entry (through the floor, over a cable vault or trench) or top entry (cable tray), and confirm whether the house sits on piles, a slab or skids. The foundation drawings must match the house's support points and arrive before the house does.

### 5. Certification and approvals

The electrical equipment inside will carry its own certification. Ask the supplier how the **assembled e-house** will be approved in your province, whether as a certified assembly or through a field evaluation, and whether your authority having jurisdiction treats it as equipment or as a building. Settle this before fabrication, not when it lands on site.

### 6. Climate

For −40 °C sites, specify insulation, vapour barriers, heater redundancy, cold-rated gaskets and door hardware, and low-temperature steel where needed. Our free [cold-climate checklist](/resources/cold-climate-checklist) lists the items to check.

## Testing an e-house

The biggest advantage of an e-house is that the whole system can be tested before it ships. A thorough **factory acceptance test** covers:

- Point-to-point wiring checks and insulation resistance
- Power-on tests of every panel, relay and control circuit
- Protection relay testing and settings checks
- Interlocks, transfer schemes and SCADA points
- HVAC, lighting, fire detection and emergency systems

Witnessing the FAT is worth the trip. Our free [FAT and commissioning pack](/resources/fat-commissioning-pack) has checklists you can bring along. After delivery, **site acceptance testing** confirms nothing shifted in transport, followed by energization and commissioning.

## Is an e-house right for your project?

An e-house usually makes sense when one or more of these apply:

- The site is remote, cold or short of skilled electrical labour
- The schedule is tight and civil works can run in parallel
- The electrical scope is large and repeated across sites
- The equipment may move to another site later

For a small electrical room in an ordinary building close to a city, a site-built room is often simpler.

## Frequently asked questions

### What does e-house stand for?

Electrical house. It is also called a power house, power distribution centre (PDC), modular substation or prefabricated electrical room.

### How long does it take to build an e-house?

Typically several months to over a year from approved drawings, depending on size and equipment. Medium-voltage switchgear and transformers are usually the longest-lead items inside.

### Is an e-house cheaper than a site-built electrical room?

The equipment and building can cost about the same or more. The savings usually come from shorter schedules, less site labour and fewer commissioning problems, especially at remote sites.

### Can transformers go inside an e-house?

Dry-type transformers often do. Large oil-filled transformers usually sit outside on their own pads for fire-safety and cooling reasons.

### How is an e-house shipped?

By truck as one or more modules within road size and weight limits, and in the North by winter road, barge or sealift. Large houses are split into modules and joined on site.
