**Temperature rise is how much hotter, on average, a transformer's windings may run above the surrounding air when carrying full rated load.** A 65 °C rise liquid-filled transformer may run its windings 65 °C above a 30 °C average ambient. The rating, together with the insulation class, sets how hard the insulation works, and insulation temperature is the biggest single factor in how long a transformer lives.

## The three temperatures that matter

1. **Ambient**: the cooling air. Standards assume an average of 30 °C over 24 hours and a maximum of 40 °C.
2. **Average winding rise**: the rated temperature rise, measured during the factory heat-run (temperature rise) test.
3. **Hottest-spot rise**: the hottest point inside the winding, higher than the average. It is the hot spot that ages the insulation.

For a 65 °C rise liquid-filled transformer, the hottest-spot rise is 80 °C. Add a 30 °C ambient and the hot spot reaches 110 °C, the design limit for thermally upgraded paper.

## Liquid-filled ratings: 55 °C and 65 °C

| Rating | Typical use |
|---|---|
| 55 °C rise | Older standard; more thermal margin |
| 65 °C rise | Current standard for most distribution and power units |
| 55/65 °C rise | Rated at 55 °C rise, with about 12% extra capacity allowed at 65 °C rise |

A 55/65 °C dual rating effectively gives you a built-in overload margin, useful where load growth is uncertain.

Units filled with **natural ester fluid** can be designed for higher temperatures because the fluid slows paper ageing. See [mineral oil vs natural ester](/blog/mineral-oil-vs-natural-ester-fr3).

## Dry-type ratings: 80 °C, 115 °C and 150 °C

Ventilated dry-type transformers usually use a **220 °C insulation class** system and are offered at three rises:

| Rise | Hot-spot margin | Typical choice |
|---|---|---|
| 150 °C | Lowest | Standard, lowest first cost |
| 115 °C | Moderate | Better efficiency, about 15% overload capacity |
| 80 °C | Highest | Lowest losses, about 30% overload capacity, longest life |

A lower-rise dry-type unit uses more copper or aluminum and steel, so it costs more up front, runs cooler, has lower load losses and handles harmonics and overloads better.

## Why temperature controls life

Paper insulation ages chemically, and the rate roughly **doubles for every 6–8 °C increase** in hot-spot temperature. Running a transformer consistently 10 °C cooler can more than double its insulation life. That is why:

- lower-rise designs last longer,
- overloading in hot weather consumes life quickly,
- moisture and oxygen in the fluid, which also speed ageing, matter so much, and
- fans, clean radiators and good ventilation pay for themselves.

## Choosing a rating

**Choose a lower rise (55 °C, or 80/115 °C dry-type) when:**

- the load is high for long hours, such as data centres or process plants,
- loads are non-linear and produce harmonics (consider [K-factor](/blog/k-factor-transformers-explained) as well),
- ambient is high, as in an electrical room without good ventilation,
- you want overload margin without a larger kVA.

**The standard rating (65 °C, or 150 °C dry-type) is fine when:**

- average loading is well below nameplate,
- the space is well ventilated,
- first cost is the main driver.

## Frequently asked questions

### What does 150 °C rise mean on a dry-type transformer?

At full load, the average winding temperature can be up to 150 °C above a 40 °C maximum ambient. It is the standard, lowest-cost dry-type rating and uses a 220 °C insulation system.

### Is an 80 °C rise transformer worth the extra cost?

Often, yes, for heavily loaded or harmonic-rich applications. It has lower load losses, about 30% overload capacity and a longer insulation life. Check payback with our [savings calculator](/resources/savings-payback-calculator).

### Does temperature rise affect efficiency?

Yes. Lower-rise designs use more conductor, which lowers resistance and load losses, so they are usually more efficient at typical loads.

### How is temperature rise tested?

By a heat-run (temperature rise) test at the factory, normally a type or special test. See our guide to [transformer testing types](/blog/transformer-testing-types).
