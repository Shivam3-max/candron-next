**A transformer is a simple machine built from a handful of parts: a steel core, two or more copper or aluminium windings, insulation, a tank of insulating fluid, and the bushings, tap changer and accessories that connect it to the outside world.** Every one of those parts affects how efficient, quiet and long-lived the unit will be. If you know what each part does, you can read a specification, a quotation or a factory test report with much more confidence.

This guide walks through a typical liquid-filled distribution or substation transformer from the inside out.

## 1. The core

The core is a stack of thin silicon-steel sheets (laminations), usually 0.23–0.30 mm thick, that carries the magnetic field between the windings. The steel is grain-oriented so magnetism flows easily along the rolling direction.

What matters:

- **Lamination quality and stacking.** Burrs, gaps or poorly staggered joints raise **no-load losses** and noise.
- **Core clamping.** The stack is held by steel frames and bolts. Loose clamping causes hum and vibration over time.
- **A single core ground.** The core is grounded at one point only. Two grounds create a circulating current and local heating.

Read more in our guide to [transformer core construction](/blog/transformer-core-construction).

## 2. The windings (coils)

The windings carry the load current. The high-voltage (HV) winding has many turns of smaller conductor, the low-voltage (LV) winding fewer turns of larger conductor. They are wound concentrically around each core leg, usually with the LV winding closest to the core.

Conductors are copper or aluminium, in round wire, rectangular strip or wide foil. Spacers and ducts between layers let fluid flow through to remove heat. The windings must be tightly braced, because a short circuit pushes the coils apart with enormous force.

Our comparison of [copper vs aluminium windings](/blog/copper-vs-aluminum-transformer-windings) covers the trade-offs.

## 3. The insulation system

Between every turn, layer and winding sits insulation: kraft paper, pressboard barriers and spacers, all impregnated with the insulating fluid. The paper is the part that ages. Its condition, driven mostly by temperature and moisture, decides how long the transformer lives.

The [temperature rise rating](/blog/transformer-temperature-rise-insulation-class) tells you how hard the insulation is allowed to work.

## 4. The insulating fluid

The fluid does two jobs: it insulates and it cools. Most units use mineral oil; many now use natural ester fluid for fire safety and environmental reasons. Fluid tests (dielectric strength, moisture, dissolved gases) are the transformer's blood test. See [mineral oil vs natural ester fluid](/blog/mineral-oil-vs-natural-ester-fr3) and our [oil test readout tool](/resources/oil-test-readout).

## 5. The tank

The steel tank holds the core, coils and fluid. It is welded, pressure-tested and painted. Small and medium units are usually **sealed** with a nitrogen or air cushion above the fluid; larger units often use a **conservator** tank. Weld quality and gasket design decide whether the tank stays leak-free for decades. See [sealed tank vs conservator](/blog/sealed-tank-vs-conservator-transformer).

## 6. Radiators and cooling equipment

Heat from the core and coils is carried by the fluid to radiators (or corrugated panels on small padmounts), which shed it to the air. Larger units add fans, and sometimes pumps, to raise the rating. The [cooling class](/blog/transformer-cooling-classes-onan-onaf), such as ONAN or ONAF, tells you which method is used.

## 7. Bushings

Bushings carry the conductors through the grounded tank wall. HV and LV bushings are sized for voltage, current and impulse level. On padmount transformers the HV side uses bushing wells and elbow connectors instead of exposed porcelain. Details are in our [transformer bushings guide](/blog/transformer-bushings-explained).

## 8. Tap changer

A tap changer adjusts the number of active turns to fine-tune output voltage, usually by ±5% in 2.5% steps on a de-energized tap changer, or across a wider range under load on an on-load tap changer. See [DETC vs OLTC](/blog/transformer-tap-changers-detc-vs-oltc).

## 9. Gauges and protective devices

Liquid level, top-fluid temperature, winding temperature, pressure-vacuum gauges, a pressure relief device and, on larger units, a sudden pressure relay or Buchholz relay. These give early warning before a problem becomes a failure. See [transformer gauges and protective devices](/blog/transformer-gauges-and-protective-devices).

## 10. Nameplate and documentation

The nameplate records ratings, impedance, connection diagram, fluid volume and weights. It is the single most useful page when you service, replace or parallel a transformer. Here is [how to read one](/blog/how-to-read-a-transformer-nameplate).

## How the parts work together

| Part | Main job | What a weak build looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Core | Carry magnetic flux | High no-load loss, loud hum |
| Windings | Carry current | Hot spots, short-circuit damage |
| Insulation | Separate voltages | Early ageing, failures |
| Fluid | Insulate and cool | Moisture, gassing, sludge |
| Tank | Contain and protect | Leaks, corrosion |
| Radiators | Shed heat | Overheating at full load |
| Bushings | Bring connections out | Flashovers, leaks |
| Tap changer | Adjust voltage | Poor contact, heating |
| Gauges | Warn early | No warning before failure |

## Frequently asked questions

### What are the main parts of a transformer?

The core, the windings, the insulation system, the insulating fluid (on liquid-filled units), the tank, the cooling equipment, the bushings, the tap changer and the protective gauges and devices.

### What part of a transformer fails most often?

Failures usually trace back to the insulation system, through overheating, moisture or a through-fault that moves the windings. Bushings and tap changers are the next most common sources.

### Do dry-type transformers have the same parts?

Mostly. They have a core, windings and insulation, but no fluid or sealed tank. They are cooled by air through a ventilated enclosure, and the insulation is a resin or varnish system instead of oil-impregnated paper.

### How can I check a transformer's build quality before it ships?

Witness the factory tests and inspect the active part where possible. Our checklist for [witnessing a transformer factory inspection](/blog/transformer-factory-witness-inspection) lists what to look at.
