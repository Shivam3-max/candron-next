**Transformer oil expands and contracts by several percent between cold nights and hot afternoons, so every liquid-filled transformer needs a way to manage that change without letting in moisture and oxygen.** Sealed-tank transformers use a cushion of gas above the oil. Conservator transformers use a separate expansion tank, usually with a rubber air bag. The choice affects maintenance, oil life and how the unit is protected.

## Why oil preservation matters

Moisture and oxygen are the two biggest enemies of transformer insulation:

- **Moisture** lowers dielectric strength and speeds paper ageing.
- **Oxygen** oxidizes oil into acids and sludge, which block cooling ducts and attack paper.

A good preservation system keeps both out for the life of the unit.

## Sealed-tank transformers

The tank is welded and gasketed airtight. A space at the top holds **dry air or nitrogen**, which compresses and expands as the oil volume changes.

**Pros**
- Simple, with no expansion tank, breather or bag to maintain.
- The oil never contacts outside air.
- Standard for distribution, padmount and many substation units up to roughly 10 MVA.

**Cons**
- Internal pressure swings with temperature, so the tank and gaskets must be strong and tight.
- A pressure-vacuum gauge is needed to confirm the seal is intact.

## Nitrogen-blanketed (inert gas) systems

A variation for larger units: a nitrogen cylinder and regulator keep a constant positive pressure above the oil. Gas is added as the oil cools and vented as it heats. These need cylinder checks and are less common today than conservators with air bags.

## Conservator transformers

A separate tank (the conservator) sits above the main tank and is connected by a pipe. The main tank stays completely full of oil; level changes happen in the conservator.

### Free-breathing conservator

The conservator breathes through a **dehydrating breather** filled with silica gel. Simple, but the oil surface still meets air, so some oxygen dissolves over time.

### Conservator with air cell (bladder or diaphragm)

A flexible rubber bag inside the conservator separates oil from air. The bag inflates and deflates through the breather. This is the most common modern arrangement on power transformers because the oil never contacts air.

**Pros of conservator designs**
- The main tank is always full, so there is no gas space and no pressure cycling in the main tank.
- Allows a **Buchholz relay** in the connecting pipe for sensitive internal-fault detection.
- Suits large units with large oil volumes.

**Cons**
- More components: conservator, bag, breather, valves.
- Silica gel must be replaced; the bag must be checked for leaks.

## Side-by-side comparison

| | Sealed tank | Conservator with air bag |
|---|---|---|
| Typical use | Distribution, padmount, smaller substation | Larger substation and power transformers |
| Oil contact with air | None | None (bag separates them) |
| Maintenance | Very low | Breather and bag checks |
| Gas-detection relay | Sudden pressure relay | Buchholz relay plus sudden pressure relay |
| Main tank pressure | Cycles with temperature | Stays near constant |

## Tank construction: what good looks like

Whatever the preservation system, the tank itself must be sound:

- **Full-penetration welds** on seams that carry pressure, with welds inspected.
- **Pressure and leak testing** at the factory, typically holding pressure for several hours with no drop.
- **Good gasket design**: nitrile or fluorocarbon gaskets in machined grooves, with controlled compression.
- **Surface preparation and coating**: blasted steel, primer and topcoat to a specified film thickness, important for Canadian winters and road salt.
- **Lifting and jacking points** rated for the full weight.

## Which should you choose?

For most distribution and medium substation applications, a **sealed tank** is the simple, low-maintenance answer. For larger units, critical loads, or where a utility standard requires it, a **conservator with an air bag** and Buchholz relay gives better long-term oil protection and fault detection.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a conservator on a transformer?

An expansion tank mounted above the main tank that absorbs changes in oil volume as temperature changes, keeping the main tank completely full of oil.

### Why do sealed transformers have a pressure gauge?

To show the gas space pressure. A gauge that changes with temperature confirms the tank is sealed; one that always reads zero suggests a leak.

### What is the rubber bag inside a conservator for?

It separates the oil from outside air, preventing oxygen and moisture from dissolving into the oil while still letting the volume change.

### Can a sealed transformer be used for large ratings?

Yes, sealed designs are used up to roughly 10 MVA and sometimes beyond, but larger units more often use conservators.
