A distribution transformer is energized 24 hours a day, every day, for decades. Even a very efficient one loses a little energy as heat — and over its life, the cost of those losses can rival the purchase price. That is why Canada, like the United States, sets **minimum efficiency levels** for distribution transformers.

This article explains the rules in plain language and how to use losses to compare quotes. Always confirm the current regulatory requirements for your product and province.

## Two kinds of loss

Every transformer has two kinds of loss:

- **No-load (core) losses.** The energy needed to magnetize the steel core. They are present whenever the transformer is energized, even with no load connected — so they run 8,760 hours a year.
- **Load (copper) losses.** Heat in the windings from the load current. They rise with the **square** of the load: at half load they are a quarter of full-load losses.

Efficiency at any load is:

`Efficiency = output ÷ (output + no-load loss + load loss)`

Because no-load losses never stop, they matter most for transformers that spend a lot of time lightly loaded — which is most building transformers.

## The Canadian rules

- **CSA C802.2** sets minimum efficiency values for **dry-type** transformers. The federal **Energy Efficiency Regulations**, administered by Natural Resources Canada (NRCan), make minimum efficiency levels mandatory for dry-type distribution transformers sold in Canada. Current levels are aligned with the US Department of Energy's 2016 levels.
- **CSA C802.1** sets minimum efficiency values for **liquid-filled** distribution transformers. Utilities and owners commonly specify it, and some jurisdictions require it.
- **CSA C802.3** covers power transformers.

Efficiency is measured at a reference load (for example 35% or 50% of nameplate, depending on the type) and reference temperature, so it reflects how transformers typically run in service rather than only at full load.

Some transformers — such as autotransformers, drive isolation and rectifier transformers, and certain special-purpose units — may be excluded or treated differently. Check the scope of the current regulation for your product.

## What it means when you buy

- Every new dry-type distribution transformer sold in Canada must meet the minimum level, so "high efficiency" on its own doesn't say much. Ask for the **actual no-load and load losses**.
- Two transformers that both meet the minimum can still differ in losses. Over 25 years that difference can be worth more than the price gap.
- Low-temperature-rise dry-type units (80 °C or 115 °C) usually have lower load losses than standard 150 °C units.

## Comparing transformers on total cost of ownership

The fair comparison is the purchase price **plus** the cost of losses over the transformer's life. A simple version:

1. Annual no-load energy = no-load loss (kW) × 8,760 h
2. Annual load energy = full-load copper loss (kW) × (average load fraction)² × hours
3. Annual cost = total energy × your electricity rate
4. Compare the difference in annual cost with the difference in price to get a simple payback

### Example

Two 300 kVA dry-type transformers both meet the regulation. Unit B has 300 W lower no-load loss and costs $1,800 more.

`0.3 kW × 8,760 h = 2,628 kWh per year`

At $0.14 per kWh, that is about **$368 a year** from no-load loss alone — a payback of about five years, on a transformer that will run for 25 or more.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are transformer efficiency standards mandatory in Canada?

Yes for dry-type distribution transformers within the scope of the federal Energy Efficiency Regulations. For liquid-filled distribution transformers, CSA C802.1 is widely specified and may be required by utilities or provinces. Confirm the requirements that apply to your product.

### What is the difference between no-load and load losses?

No-load losses magnetize the core and occur whenever the transformer is energized. Load losses are heat in the windings and rise with the square of the load current.

### Is a DOE 2016 transformer acceptable in Canada?

Canada's current minimum levels for dry-type transformers are aligned with the US DOE 2016 levels, but the product must still meet Canadian certification and labelling requirements.

### How do I compare the efficiency of two transformers?

Ask each supplier for the no-load and load losses, then compare the lifetime cost of those losses with the price difference using a total-cost-of-ownership or payback calculation.
