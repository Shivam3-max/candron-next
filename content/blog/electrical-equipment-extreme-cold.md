**Standard electrical equipment is usually rated for a lowest ambient temperature of around −30 °C, while much of northern Canada sees −40 °C to −50 °C.** In that cold, lubricants stiffen, plastics crack, batteries lose capacity, displays stop working and condensation forms every time the temperature swings. Equipment for northern, Arctic and remote sites needs a low-temperature rating stated in the specification, heaters and controls to keep it within that rating, and a shipping and spares plan that allows for long, cold supply lines.

## What actually goes wrong in the cold

| Component | Problem at very low temperature | What to specify |
|---|---|---|
| Breaker and switch mechanisms | Grease stiffens, so operating times slow down or mechanisms stick | Low-temperature lubricants; mechanism heaters; tested operating times at the minimum temperature |
| SF6 gas equipment | SF6 can liquefy at very low temperatures, reducing insulation and interrupting capability | Vacuum interrupters, gas mixtures rated for the site, or heaters, as the manufacturer specifies |
| Relays, meters and HMIs | Displays go dark or slow; electronics drift outside their rated range | Components rated to the site minimum, or heated compartments |
| Batteries (UPS, switchgear DC, generators) | Capacity drops sharply, and lead-acid batteries can freeze when discharged | Heated battery rooms or enclosures; oversize for capacity at temperature |
| Cables | Jackets become brittle; pulling and bending in the cold cracks them | Cable rated for the minimum *installation* temperature, or warm the reels before pulling |
| Gaskets and seals | Rubber hardens and stops sealing | Silicone or other low-temperature gaskets |
| Liquid-filled transformers | Oil thickens, so cooling is slower until it warms up | Fluid and design rated for the site minimum; confirm the cold-start procedure |
| Steel enclosures and structures | Ordinary steel becomes less tough | Low-temperature, impact-tested steel where the structure is load-bearing |

## Heat, humidity and condensation

The biggest everyday problem is often not the cold itself but **condensation**. Equipment that warms up during the day or under load, then cools at night, draws in moist air that condenses on cold metal and insulation. Over time that causes tracking, corrosion and flashovers.

Specify:

- **Space heaters** in every switchgear section, control compartment and transformer cabinet, with a **thermostat and humidistat**, so they run when needed and not otherwise
- **Heater monitoring** (a current relay or alarm), because a failed heater is invisible until the damage is done
- **Insulated enclosures** or a heated building (an **e-house**) for sensitive equipment
- **Breathers and vents** designed to keep snow and ice out while still allowing ventilation

## Snow, ice and wind

- Louvres and vents need hoods or filters against wind-driven snow, which can pack into enclosures through surprisingly small gaps.
- Door hardware, hinges and padlock hasps must work with gloves on, and must not freeze shut.
- Outdoor switchgear and padmounts should sit high enough to stay above drifting snow, with access cleared all winter.

## Shipping and installation in the North

Remote sites have narrow delivery windows:

- **Winter roads** are typically open for only a few weeks to a couple of months, depending on the year.
- **Arctic sealift** sails in summer and early fall, so equipment that misses the last sailing waits a year.
- **Air freight** is possible for small items but very expensive.

Build the delivery window into the schedule from the start, and work backwards from it with the [long-lead equipment planner](/resources/lead-time-planner). Factory testing matters more than usual, because fixing a problem on site may need parts that cannot arrive for months. Pack spare fuses, relays, heaters and lubricants for the first year.

Installation in the cold has its own rules: warm cable reels before pulling, keep equipment heaters energized from temporary power during construction, and leave enclosures sealed until the heaters are running.

## Why an e-house is common in the North

For larger northern projects, the electrical equipment is often installed in a **heated, insulated e-house** built and tested in the south. That moves most of the cold-sensitive gear into a controlled environment, cuts site labour and allows a full factory acceptance test. Our article on [what an e-house is](/blog/what-is-an-e-house) covers how they are designed and shipped.

## A short specification checklist

- State the **minimum and maximum ambient temperatures** for storage, operation and installation.
- Ask for the **lowest rated operating temperature** of every major component, including relays, batteries and gaskets.
- Require **heaters with thermostats, humidistats and failure alarms**.
- Ask whether breaker operating times have been **tested at the minimum temperature**.
- Specify **cable** rated for the minimum installation temperature.
- Plan delivery around **winter road or sealift windows**, with a factory acceptance test before shipment.
- List **first-year spares**.

## Frequently asked questions

### What temperature is standard switchgear rated for?

Many standard designs are rated for a minimum ambient of about −30 °C. Equipment for colder sites needs a low-temperature rating, heaters, or installation in a heated building.

### Why do switchgear heaters need thermostats and humidistats?

To control condensation efficiently. Heaters running all the time waste energy and can overheat components; heaters that never run let moisture condense.

### Can transformers be energized after sitting at −40 °C?

Most distribution transformers can, but liquid-filled units cool more slowly until the oil warms. Follow the manufacturer's cold-start guidance, especially before applying full load.

### What cable should I use in extreme cold?

Use cable rated for the site's minimum installation temperature, not just its operating temperature, or warm the reels before pulling. Many jackets crack if bent in severe cold.

### Is an e-house better for northern sites?

Often, yes. It keeps sensitive equipment heated and dry, moves most of the work to a factory and allows full testing before a long, expensive delivery.
