**For high-current feeders (roughly 1,600 A and above) running more than a few metres, busway is usually smaller, faster to install and has lower voltage drop than the many parallel cables needed to match it. Cable usually wins on short runs, lower currents, irregular routes, and when the route may change late in the job.** The right answer depends on current, run length, route, schedule and how often the building's loads will move.

The decision usually comes up in two places: the **transformer-to-main-switchboard connection** and **vertical risers** that feed several floors.

## What busway is

Busway (also called bus duct) is a set of insulated copper or aluminum bars in a steel or aluminum housing, supplied in factory-made straight lengths, elbows and fittings that bolt together on site.

- **Feeder busway** carries power from A to B with no taps, for example from a transformer to a switchboard.
- **Plug-in busway** has openings along its length where fused switches or breakers ("plug-in units") can be inserted, which is common in risers, data centres and manufacturing plants where loads move.

Typical ratings run from about 225 A to 5,000 A at 600 V and below. Medium-voltage bus duct also exists for connecting transformers to MV switchgear.

## The cable alternative

A 4,000 A feeder in cable means many conductors in parallel. With 500 kcmil copper (about 380 A each at 75 °C before derating), that is roughly **11 parallel sets**, or 33 phase conductors plus neutrals and grounds, all in conduit or on tray. Each set must be the same length and type so the current shares equally, and grouping derates their ampacity further.

## Side-by-side comparison

| Factor | Busway | Parallel cable |
|---|---|---|
| Space | Compact; one housing | Large tray or many conduits |
| Install time | Fast: bolt sections together | Slow: pull, terminate and test many sets |
| Voltage drop | Low, with low and balanced reactance | Higher on long runs; can be unbalanced if sets are not identical |
| Flexibility to add loads | High with plug-in busway | Low: a new feeder means a new cable run |
| Route changes | Poor: lengths are made to measured dimensions | Good: cable can be re-routed on site |
| Material cost | Higher per metre at low currents | Lower at low currents and short runs |
| Lead time | Longer: made to order from field dimensions | Usually shorter, cable often from stock |
| Maintenance | Joint torque checks and infrared scans | Termination checks; mostly maintenance-free in between |
| Fire stopping | Rated fittings where it passes through floors | Standard firestop for cable and conduit |
| Moisture | Needs the right rating (indoor/outdoor, sprinkler-proof) | Depends on cable type |

## Where busway clearly wins

- **Transformer to main switchboard above about 1,600–2,000 A**, especially with a throat connection or close-coupled layout.
- **Risers in multi-storey buildings**, with plug-in units on each floor.
- **Data centres and plants** where loads move and new taps are needed without an outage on the whole feeder.
- **Tight electrical rooms** where tray space is not available.

## Where cable clearly wins

- **Runs under about 1,000 A**, where a few cable sets are cheap and simple.
- **Underground or outdoor routes** between buildings.
- **Complex or uncertain routes**, especially in renovations where field dimensions cannot be confirmed before ordering.
- **Fast-track jobs** where busway lead time would delay energization.

## Worked comparison: 3,000 A, 25 m run

A 2,500 kVA, 600 V transformer (secondary full load 2,406 A) feeds a 3,000 A main switchboard 25 m away.

- **Cable:** about 8 parallel sets of 500 kcmil copper (8 × 380 A = 3,040 A before derating). Grouping derating may push this to 9 or 10 sets. That is 27–30 phase conductors plus neutrals: several days of pulling and terminating, and a large tray.
- **Busway:** one 3,000 A copper feeder run with two elbows and flanged ends. Installation is typically a fraction of the cable labour, and voltage drop is lower.

Here busway is usually the better choice, provided the route is fixed early and field-measured before release. Figures are indicative; size every feeder with the project's actual ambient, grouping and code tables.

## Specifying busway well

1. **Field-measure before release.** Busway is made to measured lengths; a wrong dimension means waiting for a new section.
2. **Match the flanges** to the transformer throat and switchboard entry: coordinate drawings between suppliers.
3. **State the short-circuit rating** (kA) to match the available fault current.
4. **Choose copper or aluminum** and the enclosure rating (indoor, outdoor, sprinkler-proof).
5. **Plan maintenance access** for joint torque checks and annual infrared scans.
6. **Check lead time** against the schedule with the [long-lead equipment planner](/resources/lead-time-planner).

## Frequently asked questions

### Is busway cheaper than cable?
At low currents and short runs, cable is usually cheaper. Above roughly 1,600–2,000 A and on longer runs, busway is often cheaper overall once labour, tray and space are included.

### Does busway need maintenance?
Yes. Bolted joints should be checked by infrared thermography (typically annually) and re-torqued as the manufacturer recommends. Many modern designs use single-bolt joints with indicator washers to make checks easier.

### Can busway be used outdoors?
Yes, with an outdoor-rated housing and proper weather seals. Indoor busway must not be exposed to water unless it carries a sprinkler-proof or similar rating.

### What is the difference between feeder and plug-in busway?
Feeder busway has no tap-off points and only carries power between two points. Plug-in busway has openings where fused switches or breakers can be plugged in to feed loads along the run.

### Why does busway have lower voltage drop than cable?
The bars are closely spaced and arranged to minimise reactance, and the conductors are large and short. Parallel cable sets, especially if not perfectly matched, have higher and less balanced impedance.
