**Trade rules for electrical equipment between Canada and the United States changed several times in 2026.** The US now charges its Section 232 metal tariffs on the **full value** of covered goods, at rates from 10% to 50% depending on metal content and origin. Canada has applied **counter-tariffs of 15%, 25% or 50%** on a list of US products. For a Canadian buyer, the practical effect depends on where the equipment and its metals come from. Origin, metal content and tariff terms now belong in every quote comparison.

*This update reflects published government information as of early November 2026. Tariff rules change often and this is not legal or customs advice. Check the current rules for your specific products with a customs broker.*

## What changed on the US side

| Date | Change |
|---|---|
| August 2025 | The US extended Section 232 tariffs to imported copper and copper-intensive products |
| 6 April 2026 | Section 232 tariffs on steel, aluminum, copper and derivative products restructured into tiers, charged on the **full value** of the goods rather than only on their metal content |
| 2026 | US Executive Order 14420 restricts imports of bulk-power equipment from certain countries, with China the largest affected |
| 15 September 2026 | According to the Government of Canada, US Section 338 tariffs began stacking on top of Section 232 tariffs |

Under the April 2026 structure, as summarized by the Government of Canada:

- Goods with **less than 15% applicable metal content** by weight are exempt.
- Goods with **at least 85% US-origin** steel, aluminum or copper content get a reduced **10%** rate.
- Other covered goods face rates **up to 50%**, charged on their full value.

Transformers and switchgear contain a lot of steel, copper and often aluminum, so many are covered. How a specific product is treated depends on its tariff classification, metal content and origin. Ask the exporter or a customs broker to confirm.

## What changed on the Canadian side

In September 2026, Canada applied **counter-tariffs of 15%, 25% or 50%** on a list of US products, with rates generally matching the corresponding US rate. Steel and aluminum products are among them. Some existing remission (refund) arrangements were carried over.

For Canadian buyers, this is the part that bites directly: **US-made equipment imported into Canada may carry a Canadian surtax** if its tariff classification is on the counter-measures list. Check the list for the specific tariff codes of the equipment you are buying, and whether a remission applies.

## US import restrictions on grid equipment

Executive Order 14420 restricts US imports of bulk-power equipment from certain countries. Wood Mackenzie estimated in August 2026 that it could affect more than US$22 billion of imports since 2025, and said it is likely to worsen the US transformer shortage. It does not apply to Canadian purchases directly. But it pushes US buyers toward North American factories, which tightens supply for Canadian projects too. See our update on [the transformer shortage](/blog/transformer-shortage-canada-2026).

## Five questions to ask on every quote

1. **Where is it made, and where do the major materials come from?** Country of manufacture, plus origin of the core steel, windings and enclosure.
2. **Which tariff codes apply, and are any duties or surtaxes included in the price?** Get it in writing.
3. **Who carries tariff risk if rates change between order and delivery?** Many suppliers now add tariff-adjustment clauses. Agree the trigger, the cap and the evidence required.
4. **What are the Incoterms?** They decide who is the importer of record and who pays duties at the border.
5. **How long is the price valid?** With tariffs and copper both moving, long validity has real value.

## Where Canadian-made equipment fits

For a Canadian project, equipment built in Canada from domestically sourced or tariff-free materials avoids the cross-border question entirely. There is no US export, no Canadian surtax on a US import, and no border delay. That is not automatically cheaper, because materials are priced globally. But it removes a source of cost and schedule uncertainty. That is one reason CANDRON focuses on [Canadian-made equipment](/manufacturing).

If you export equipment to US projects, the reverse applies: Section 232 and stacked US tariffs may now apply to Canadian-made goods with significant metal content, and rules of origin matter more than before.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do US tariffs apply to transformers made in Canada?

Canadian-made transformers exported to the US can fall under Section 232 metal tariffs because of their steel and copper content. The rate depends on classification, metal content and origin. Confirm with a customs broker.

### Does Canada charge tariffs on US electrical equipment?

Canada applied counter-tariffs of 15%, 25% or 50% on a list of US products in September 2026. Whether a particular piece of equipment is affected depends on whether its tariff code is on the list.

### What changed with Section 232 in April 2026?

The tariffs were restructured into tiers based on metal content and origin, and are charged on the full value of the goods. Goods under 15% metal content are exempt, and goods with at least 85% US-origin metal pay 10%.

### How can I protect a project from tariff changes?

Ask suppliers to state origin and included duties, agree tariff-adjustment terms up front, choose clear Incoterms, and consider domestically made equipment for Canadian projects.
