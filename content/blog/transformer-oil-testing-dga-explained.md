The fluid inside a liquid-filled transformer is more than insulation and coolant. It is also the best window into the transformer's health. A small sample, sent to a lab once a year, can show moisture creeping in, insulation ageing and — most importantly — a fault developing inside the tank long before it fails.

This guide explains what is on a typical oil test report and how engineers read it.

## What a typical oil test report includes

| Test | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Dissolved gas analysis (DGA) | Whether a fault is developing, and what kind |
| Moisture (water content) | How wet the insulation is; drives ageing and dielectric strength |
| Dielectric breakdown voltage | The fluid's ability to withstand voltage |
| Acidity (neutralization number) | Oxidation and ageing of the oil |
| Interfacial tension | Contamination and oxidation products |
| Colour and visual | A quick check on condition |
| Furans | Ageing of the paper insulation |
| Power factor / dissipation factor | Contamination and deterioration |

## Dissolved gas analysis: the key gases

When oil and paper insulation are stressed by heat or electrical discharge, they break down into gases that dissolve in the oil. Which gases appear, and in what proportions, points to the type of fault.

| Gas | What it usually points to |
|---|---|
| Hydrogen (H₂) | Almost any fault; on its own, partial discharge |
| Methane (CH₄) | Low-temperature overheating of oil |
| Ethane (C₂H₆) | Overheating of oil, below about 300 °C |
| Ethylene (C₂H₄) | Hotter overheating; rises quickly with temperature |
| Acetylene (C₂H₂) | Arcing or very high temperatures — take it seriously |
| Carbon monoxide (CO) | Paper insulation ageing or overheating |
| Carbon dioxide (CO₂) | Normal paper ageing; a low CO₂/CO ratio can point to paper overheating |

**Total dissolved combustible gas (TDCG)** — the sum of hydrogen, methane, ethane, ethylene, acetylene and carbon monoxide — is a quick overall indicator.

## Levels matter, but trends matter more

A single result is a snapshot. Engineers look at:

- **Absolute levels** compared with typical values for transformers of similar age and type, using guidance such as IEEE C57.104
- **Rate of increase** between samples — a gas that is rising quickly matters more than a high but stable one
- **Ratios between gases**, which point to the fault type

That is why the second and third samples are often more useful than the first. Always keep your history and send it with each new result.

## The Duval Triangle

The **Duval Triangle** is a widely used way to classify the fault type from three gases: methane, ethylene and acetylene. Their relative percentages plot as a point inside a triangle divided into zones:

- **PD** — partial discharge
- **T1** — thermal fault below 300 °C
- **T2** — thermal fault 300–700 °C
- **T3** — thermal fault above 700 °C
- **D1** — low-energy discharge (sparking)
- **D2** — high-energy discharge (arcing)
- **DT** — a mix of thermal and electrical faults

The triangle tells you what kind of fault the gases look like, not whether there is one. A healthy transformer with low gas levels can plot anywhere, so use it only when levels or rates of increase are above normal. ## Moisture, breakdown voltage and acidity

- **Moisture** is the enemy of insulation. Wet paper ages faster and lowers dielectric strength. Rising moisture often means a seal or breather problem.
- **Breakdown voltage** falls with moisture and particles. A low result calls for filtering or drying the oil.
- **Acidity** rises as oil oxidizes. Acids attack the paper and form sludge that blocks cooling. High acidity is a sign to reclaim or replace the oil.

## Furans: the age of the paper

Paper insulation cannot be sampled without opening the transformer, but as it ages it releases **furans** into the oil. Furan results give an estimate of how much life is left in the paper, which is useful when deciding between repair and replacement.

## How often to sample

- **Annually** for most distribution and power transformers
- **More often** for critical units, units with rising gases, and after a fault, a through-fault or an overload event
- **Before and after** major work, and as a baseline after commissioning

Good sampling technique matters: the sample must be airtight, or gases escape and air gets in.

## Natural ester fluids

Natural ester fluids such as FR3 produce gases differently from mineral oil, and some limits and interpretation methods are different. Tell the lab and the engineer which fluid is in the transformer.

## Frequently asked questions

### What does acetylene in transformer oil mean?

Acetylene points to arcing or very high temperatures. Any measurable and increasing acetylene deserves prompt attention: retest, compare with the trend and get an engineering opinion.

### How often should transformer oil be tested?

Once a year is typical. Sample more often for critical transformers, if gases are rising, or after a fault or overload.

### What is a normal DGA result?

It depends on the transformer's age, design, fluid and loading. Engineers compare results with typical values (for example from IEEE C57.104) and, more importantly, with the transformer's own trend.
