**Metal-clad switchgear uses draw-out circuit breakers, each in its own grounded steel compartment, with every major part (breaker, bus, cables, controls) separated by metal barriers. Metal-enclosed switchgear is simpler. It usually uses fixed load-interrupter switches and fuses inside a steel enclosure, with less internal separation.** Metal-clad costs more and takes more space, but gives relay-based protection, faster maintenance and higher continuity of service. Metal-enclosed is a cost-effective way to switch and protect transformers and feeders where breakers and relays are not needed.

Both are medium-voltage equipment, typically **5 kV to 38 kV** in Canada (4.16, 13.8, 25 and 27.6 kV are common system voltages).

## The short comparison

| | Metal-clad | Metal-enclosed (load-interrupter) |
|---|---|---|
| Main device | Draw-out vacuum circuit breaker | Fixed load-interrupter switch, usually with power fuses |
| Protection | Protective relays: overcurrent, ground fault, differential and more | Fuses |
| Compartments | Grounded metal barriers between breaker, bus, cable and control sections | Fewer barriers; often a single compartment per section |
| Main bus | Insulated | May be bare |
| Shutters | Automatic shutters cover live contacts when the breaker is withdrawn | Not required |
| Automatic reclosing / transfer | Yes | Limited; switches can be motorized |
| Footprint and cost | Larger, higher cost | Smaller, lower cost |
| Typical standard | IEEE C37.20.2 | IEEE C37.20.3 |

In Canada, medium-voltage switchgear assemblies are also certified to CSA C22.2 No. 31.

## Metal-clad: when you need breakers and relays

Metal-clad switchgear is the choice when the system needs:

- **Coordinated protection** with relays that can be set and tested, such as feeder overcurrent, ground fault, bus differential and transformer differential
- **Frequent switching**, because breakers are rated for many more operations than fused switches
- **Automatic transfer** between two utility feeds or between utility and generator, for example a main-tie-main lineup
- **Remote control and SCADA** for utilities, data centres, hospitals and large plants
- **Maintenance without a full outage**: a breaker can be racked out and replaced with a spare while the rest of the lineup stays live

The draw-out design and metal barriers are what "metal-clad" really means. A fault in one compartment is much less likely to spread to the next.

## Metal-enclosed: switching and protecting transformers

Metal-enclosed load-interrupter switchgear suits jobs where you mainly need to **switch a feeder or a transformer on and off, and protect it with fuses**. Common uses:

- The primary switch and fuse in front of a **unit substation transformer**
- Service entrance switching for a commercial building or small plant
- Sectionalizing on a private medium-voltage loop

It is cheaper and smaller, and fuses are fast and simple. The trade-offs: a blown fuse must be replaced (and all three should be replaced together), coordination is less flexible than with relays, and you cannot reclose automatically.

## Pad-mounted switchgear

**Pad-mounted switchgear** is a third family. It is low-profile, tamper-resistant and dead-front, and built for **outdoor underground distribution**: subdivisions, campuses, solar and wind collector systems, and utility loops. It typically uses load-break switches, fuses or vacuum fault interrupters, and it follows IEEE C37.74.

## Where arc-resistant switchgear fits

Either metal-clad or metal-enclosed gear can be supplied **arc-resistant**, meaning it has been tested to redirect the hot gas and pressure of an internal arc away from people standing in front of or around it. Testing follows IEEE C37.20.7, which defines accessibility types:

- **Type 1** — protection at the front only
- **Type 2** — front, sides and back
- **Type 2B** — Type 2, plus protection with the low-voltage instrument compartment door open

Arc-resistant gear needs a path to vent the arc gases, such as plenums or ducts, so the room layout must allow for it. Read more in our guide to [arc flash and CSA Z462](/blog/arc-flash-csa-z462).

## How to choose

| If you need… | Choose |
|---|---|
| Relay protection, automatic transfer, SCADA, high continuity | Metal-clad |
| Simple switching and fused protection of a transformer | Metal-enclosed load-interrupter |
| Outdoor underground loop or collector system | Pad-mounted switchgear |
| Higher personnel protection where people work near energized gear | Arc-resistant version of the above |

Many projects use more than one: metal-clad for the main incoming lineup, with load-interrupter or pad-mounted gear further out on the system. Equipment rated at 25 or 27.6 kV with arc-resistant construction and long relay lists is among the longest-lead items on a project, so it is worth releasing early. ## Frequently asked questions

### What is the main difference between metal-clad and metal-enclosed switchgear?

Metal-clad uses draw-out circuit breakers in separately grounded metal compartments, with relays for protection. Metal-enclosed usually uses fixed fused load-interrupter switches with fewer internal barriers.

### Is metal-clad switchgear safer?

Its compartmentalization, shutters and draw-out breakers reduce the chance of contacting live parts and of a fault spreading. Either type can be bought arc-resistant for added protection against internal arcs.

### What voltage is metal-clad switchgear?

It is typically used from about 5 kV to 38 kV, covering common Canadian distribution voltages such as 4.16, 13.8, 25 and 27.6 kV.

### Can metal-enclosed switchgear use circuit breakers?

Some metal-enclosed designs use fixed-mounted breakers, but they do not meet all the metal-clad requirements, such as draw-out construction and full compartmentalization.

### Which costs less?

Metal-enclosed load-interrupter switchgear usually costs less and takes less floor space than a metal-clad lineup with the same number of sections.
