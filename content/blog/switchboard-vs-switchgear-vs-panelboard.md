"Switchboard" and "switchgear" are often used as if they meant the same thing, and panelboards get lumped in too. They are related — all three take power in and share it out through protective devices — but they are built to different standards, handle different currents and fault levels, and suit different jobs. Mixing them up in a specification causes expensive surprises at the submittal stage.

## The one-line summary

- **Panelboard:** the last stop. Small branch breakers feeding lighting, receptacles and small equipment.
- **Switchboard:** the building's main distribution point at low voltage. Free-standing, front-accessible, with moulded-case or insulated-case breakers.
- **Switchgear:** heavier-duty distribution with compartmentalized construction and draw-out power circuit breakers, at low voltage or medium voltage, built for high fault levels, selectivity and maintenance without a full shutdown.

## Panelboards

A panelboard is a single enclosure, usually wall-mounted, with a bus and many small branch breakers. Typical mains run from about 100 A to 1,200 A. Panelboards are what most people picture as "the electrical panel".

- **Standard:** CSA C22.2 No. 29 (and UL 67 in the US)
- **Devices:** bolt-on or plug-in moulded-case branch breakers
- **Access:** front only, usually in a wall or on a backboard
- **Use:** branch circuits at 120/208 V or 347/600 V

## Switchboards

A switchboard is a free-standing assembly of one or more vertical sections, usually from about 400 A to 4,000 A at 600 V and below. It is often the service entrance — where the utility hands over — and feeds panelboards, motor control centres, chillers and other large loads.

- **Standard:** CSA C22.2 No. 244, harmonized with UL 891
- **Devices:** moulded-case or insulated-case breakers, often group-mounted on a common panel, with fixed or draw-out main breakers
- **Construction:** dead-front, but the bus and cable areas behind it are generally not separated by barriers
- **Access:** front-accessible; larger units may need rear access to cables and bus
- **Use:** service entrance and main distribution in commercial, institutional and light industrial buildings

Switchboards are the economical choice for most buildings. A [service-entrance switchboard](/products/switchboards/main-breaker) with metering and ground-fault protection covers a large share of projects.

## Low-voltage switchgear

Low-voltage switchgear looks similar from the front but is built differently inside. Each breaker sits in its own compartment, and the bus and cable areas are separated.

- **Standard:** CSA C22.2 No. 31 and IEEE C37.20.1 (UL 1558 in the US)
- **Devices:** draw-out low-voltage power circuit breakers that can be racked out for testing and maintenance
- **Ratings:** typically up to 5,000–6,000 A and high short-circuit ratings
- **Strengths:** better selective coordination (short-time ratings let downstream breakers clear faults first), easier maintenance and replacement of a breaker without a full shutdown

Choose [LV switchgear](/products/lv-switchgear) for data centres, hospitals, large plants and anywhere uptime and maintainability matter more than first cost.

## Medium-voltage switchgear

Medium-voltage switchgear works above 1,000 V — typically 4.16 kV to 34.5 kV in Canada. The main types are:

- **Metal-clad:** draw-out vacuum circuit breakers in grounded steel compartments, with automatic shutters and protective relays. Built to IEEE C37.20.2.
- **Metal-enclosed load-interrupter:** fused load-break switches. Simpler and lower cost where breakers and relays aren't needed.
- **Arc-resistant:** metal-clad gear tested to contain an internal arc and vent it away from the operator (IEEE C37.20.7).
- **Pad-mounted:** outdoor, tamper-resistant gear for underground distribution loops.

## How to choose

1. **Voltage.** Above 600 V you are in medium-voltage switchgear territory.
2. **Fault current.** Check the available fault current with the [fault current calculator](/resources/fault-current-calculator). Very high fault levels push you towards switchgear.
3. **Uptime and maintenance.** If a breaker must be tested or replaced without shutting the building down, choose draw-out switchgear.
4. **Selectivity.** Hospitals, data centres and critical facilities often need short-time ratings that switchgear provides.
5. **Space and budget.** Switchboards are smaller and cheaper for the same ampacity.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a switchboard the same as switchgear?

No. Both distribute power, but low-voltage switchgear uses compartmentalized construction and draw-out power circuit breakers, and is built to a different standard (CSA C22.2 No. 31 rather than No. 244). It costs more and is easier to maintain without a full shutdown.

### What is the difference between a panelboard and a switchboard?

A panelboard is a wall-mounted enclosure of small branch breakers, usually up to about 1,200 A. A switchboard is a free-standing, larger assembly that feeds panelboards and big loads, typically from 400 A to 4,000 A.

### When do I need medium-voltage switchgear?

When the facility takes service from the utility above 1,000 V — for example at 13.8 kV or 27.6 kV — and distributes that voltage to transformers or large motors on site.

### Can a switchboard be service entrance equipment?

Yes. Many switchboards are labelled for service entrance use, with a main breaker, ground-fault protection where the code requires it and a utility metering section.
