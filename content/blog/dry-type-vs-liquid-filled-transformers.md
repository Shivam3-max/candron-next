Every transformer does the same basic job: two windings on a steel core change one voltage into another. What differs is how the windings are insulated and cooled. A **dry-type** transformer uses air and solid insulation. A **liquid-filled** transformer sits in a sealed tank of mineral oil or a natural ester fluid such as FR3, which both insulates and carries heat away.

That one difference decides where each type can go, how big it is, how it is maintained and what it costs over its life.

## The short answer

- **Inside a building, close to people:** dry-type is usually the default. No liquid means no spill containment and much lower fire risk.
- **Outdoors, on a pad or a pole, or at higher ratings:** liquid-filled is usually the better choice. It is smaller, cheaper per kVA at larger sizes, handles overloads well and is built for the weather.

The rest of this article explains why, and where the grey areas are.

## Side-by-side comparison

| | Dry-type | Liquid-filled |
|---|---|---|
| Insulation and cooling | Air and solid insulation (varnish, epoxy) | Mineral oil or natural ester (FR3) |
| Typical location | Indoors: electrical rooms, ceilings, near loads | Outdoors: pads, poles, substations |
| Fire risk | Low; nothing liquid to burn or leak | Higher with mineral oil; much lower with FR3 |
| Containment | Not needed | Often needed for oil; check local rules |
| Size and weight per kVA | Larger | Smaller, especially above ~500 kVA |
| Overload capacity | Limited | Good; the fluid absorbs short overloads |
| Noise | Can be louder | Usually quieter |
| Maintenance | Cleaning, connection checks, infrared scans | Plus regular fluid sampling and testing |
| Condition monitoring | Visual and thermal | Oil tests and dissolved gas analysis tell you a lot |
| Typical ratings | Up to a few MVA, up to about 35–46 kV class | From a few kVA to hundreds of MVA |

## Fire safety and location decide most projects

Inside a building, the main concern is fire. A mineral-oil transformer indoors usually needs a fire-rated vault, containment for the oil and more attention from the building and insurance side. A dry-type transformer avoids all of that, which is why almost every transformer inside commercial, institutional and light industrial buildings in Canada is dry-type.

Outdoors the picture flips. A sealed, welded tank shrugs off rain, snow, dust and salt, so padmount, polemount and substation transformers are nearly always liquid-filled.

**Natural ester fluid (FR3)** narrows the gap. It has a much higher fire point than mineral oil and is biodegradable, so it is popular near buildings, in environmentally sensitive sites and where insurers prefer it. One thing to check in Canada: natural esters have a higher pour point than mineral oil, so ask how the transformer will behave after a cold start at your minimum site temperature. ## Size, cost and efficiency

Fluid carries heat away far better than air. A liquid-filled transformer of the same kVA is therefore smaller and lighter, and above roughly 500 kVA it is usually cheaper to buy as well.

Both types can be very efficient. In Canada, minimum efficiency levels apply — dry-type units are covered by CSA C802.2 and the federal Energy Efficiency Regulations, and liquid-filled distribution transformers by CSA C802.1. Losses matter more than many buyers think: a transformer runs 24 hours a day for decades, so a few hundred watts of extra loss adds up. ## Maintenance and life

A **dry-type** transformer needs little: keep it clean and ventilated, check connections and run an infrared scan under load. The catch is that you cannot see inside the insulation, so problems often show up late.

A **liquid-filled** transformer needs regular fluid sampling, but that sample is also its biggest advantage. Moisture, acidity and dissolved gas analysis show how the insulation is ageing and whether a fault is developing, long before it fails. See [how to read a transformer oil test](/blog/transformer-oil-testing-dga-explained).

## Which dry-type construction?

If you choose dry-type, there are a few constructions:

- **Ventilated (VPI or VPE):** the windings are vacuum-pressure impregnated with varnish or epoxy. This is the standard choice for clean indoor rooms.
- **Cast coil:** each winding is cast in solid epoxy. It shrugs off moisture, dust and salt and can be energized after a long idle period without a dry-out, which suits damp, dirty or standby duty.
- **Encapsulated:** small transformers fully potted in compound, for outdoor, dusty or hazardous-location duty.

## A simple decision guide

1. **Is it inside an occupied building?** Start with dry-type.
2. **Is it outdoors, on a pad or a pole?** Start with liquid-filled.
3. **Is it above a few MVA or above 35 kV?** Liquid-filled is usually the only practical choice.
4. **Is fire risk or the environment a special concern outdoors?** Consider natural ester (FR3) fluid.
5. **Is the site damp, dusty or salty, or will the unit sit idle?** Consider cast coil dry-type.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can an oil-filled transformer be installed indoors?

Yes, but it usually needs a fire-rated vault, oil containment and approval from the authority having jurisdiction. That is why indoor transformers in Canada are almost always dry-type, or liquid-filled with a less-flammable fluid.

### Which lasts longer, dry-type or liquid-filled?

Both can last decades when loaded sensibly and maintained. Liquid-filled transformers have the advantage that oil testing shows how the insulation is ageing, so problems are easier to catch early.

### Is a dry-type transformer more expensive?

At small and medium ratings the prices are close. Above roughly 500 kVA, liquid-filled transformers are usually cheaper per kVA, but installation costs for containment or a vault can change the comparison indoors.

### What is FR3 fluid?

FR3 is a brand of natural ester insulating fluid made from vegetable oil. It has a much higher fire point than mineral oil, is biodegradable and can extend the life of the paper insulation.
