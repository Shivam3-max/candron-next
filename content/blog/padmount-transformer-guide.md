A **padmount transformer** is the green (or grey) steel cabinet you see on a concrete pad outside commercial buildings, plants, solar farms and housing developments. It takes medium-voltage power from an underground utility or site cable — typically 4.16 kV to 34.5 kV in Canada — and steps it down to a utilization voltage such as 600Y/347 V or 208Y/120 V.

Inside the cabinet is a sealed tank of mineral oil or natural ester fluid holding the core and windings, with separate, lockable compartments for the high-voltage and low-voltage connections.

## Standard sizes

Three-phase padmounts are commonly built in these ratings:

`75, 112.5, 150, 225, 300, 500, 750, 1,000, 1,500, 2,000, 2,500, 3,000, 3,750 and 5,000 kVA`

Larger units are available, and single-phase padmounts (typically 10 to 250 kVA) serve residential subdivisions. Use the [kVA calculator](/resources/kva-calculator) and our guide on [how to size a transformer](/blog/how-to-size-a-transformer) to pick the rating.

## Loop feed vs radial feed

This is about how the primary cable arrives.

- **Radial feed:** one primary cable comes in and ends at the transformer. Simple and lower cost, but if that cable fails, the transformer is out until it is repaired.
- **Loop feed:** two primary cables connect, so the medium-voltage circuit loops through the transformer to the next one. With a loop switch, the utility or site can isolate a faulted cable section and feed the transformer from the other direction.

Loop feed is the norm on underground utility loops and on sites with several transformers in a ring. Radial feed suits a single transformer at the end of a line.

## Dead-front vs live-front

This is about how the primary cables are terminated.

- **Dead-front:** cables connect with insulated, shielded elbows plugged into bushings. No energized metal is exposed when the door is open, and elbows can be operated as load-break connections with a hot stick. This is the modern standard and the safer choice.
- **Live-front:** cables terminate on exposed porcelain bushings with stress cones. It is an older style still used on some utility systems and replacements that must match existing installations.

## Protection: fusing and switches

Typical padmount protection includes:

- **Bay-O-Net expulsion fuses** that can be replaced from the front with a hot stick
- **Partial-range current-limiting fuses** in series, to clear high-current internal faults
- A **load-break switch** (two-position for radial, four-position or sectionalizing for loop)
- **Surge arresters** on the primary

Many Canadian utilities publish their own padmount specifications, which fix the fusing, bushing arrangement and cabinet layout. If the transformer will be utility-owned or utility-interconnected, start from that standard.

## Fluid: mineral oil or natural ester

- **Mineral oil** is the traditional, lower-cost choice.
- **Natural ester (FR3)** has a much higher fire point and is biodegradable. It is often specified near buildings, in environmentally sensitive areas and for inverter-duty units. Check behaviour at your minimum winter temperature, since natural esters have a higher pour point.

See [dry-type vs liquid-filled transformers](/blog/dry-type-vs-liquid-filled-transformers) for how fluids compare.

## Standards in Canada

Padmount transformers for Canadian projects are typically built to:

- **CSA C2.1** — single-phase and three-phase liquid-filled distribution transformers
- **CSA C227.4** — three-phase padmount transformers with separable connectors
- **CSA C802.1** — minimum efficiency values for liquid-filled distribution transformers
- **IEEE C57.12.34 and C57.12.28** — padmount requirements and enclosure integrity

A ready-to-edit specification is in our [Division 26 guide specifications](/resources/division-26-specs) (Section 26 12 19).

## What to put in your specification

1. kVA rating and cooling class
2. Primary voltage, BIL and connection (delta or grounded wye)
3. Secondary voltage
4. Loop or radial feed, dead-front or live-front
5. Fusing and switching
6. Fluid type
7. Impedance (or "per fault study")
8. Taps (typically ±2 × 2.5%)
9. Accessories: gauges, drain and sampling valve, alarm contacts
10. Cabinet finish, and stainless steel for coastal sites

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the difference between loop feed and radial feed?

A radial-feed padmount has one primary cable and is the end of the line. A loop-feed padmount has two, so the medium-voltage circuit continues to the next transformer and can be fed from either direction after a cable fault.

### Is dead-front or live-front safer?

Dead-front. All primary connections are insulated and shielded, so no energized metal is exposed when the cabinet is open.

### What size padmount transformers are available?

Three-phase padmounts are commonly available from 75 kVA to 5,000 kVA, with larger units built to order. Single-phase padmounts typically range from 10 to 250 kVA.

### How long does a padmount transformer last?

A well-maintained padmount typically serves for decades. Regular inspections and periodic oil testing are the best way to spot problems early.
