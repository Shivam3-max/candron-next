**Copper and aluminum both make reliable transformer windings when the transformer is designed for the material.** Copper conducts better, so copper windings are smaller. Aluminum is lighter and usually cheaper, so aluminum windings are larger but often lower in first cost. Performance on the nameplate, losses, impedance and temperature rise, is set by the design, not by the metal alone.

## How the two metals compare

| Property | Copper | Aluminum |
|---|---|---|
| Electrical conductivity | 100% (reference) | About 61% of copper |
| Cross-section for the same resistance | 1× | About 1.6× |
| Weight for the same resistance | 1× | About half |
| Material cost | Higher and more volatile | Lower |
| Mechanical strength | Higher | Lower |
| Coil size | More compact | Larger |

Because aluminum needs more cross-section, an aluminum-wound transformer usually has a slightly larger core window and tank. Because aluminum is so light, the finished unit can still weigh about the same or less.

## Does aluminum mean higher losses?

Not necessarily. A designer meets the guaranteed **load losses** by sizing the conductor. An aluminum design simply uses more conductor area to reach the same resistance. Two transformers built to the same loss guarantee perform the same in service, whichever metal they use.

What changes is the **balance of cost**: copper buys a smaller coil, aluminum buys a cheaper one. With copper prices high, aluminum often wins on price for distribution and dry-type units. See our note on [copper prices and transformer costs](/blog/copper-prices-transformer-costs-2026).

## Winding styles

- **Round or rectangular wire** for HV windings with many turns.
- **Foil windings** (a wide, thin sheet the full height of the coil) for LV windings. Foil spreads current evenly and withstands short-circuit forces well. Aluminum foil is very common in LV windings of both dry-type and liquid-filled units.
- **Continuously transposed cable** in large power transformers, to control stray losses.

## Short-circuit strength

During a fault, windings experience forces many times their normal load. Copper's higher strength gives more margin in compact designs. Aluminum designs handle this with proper bracing, foil construction and conservative current density. The real test is the design and manufacturing quality, verified by short-circuit design reviews or type tests on larger units.

## Terminations and joints

Most past problems with aluminum came from **connections**, not windings. Aluminum oxidizes and creeps under pressure, so joints need:

- tin-plated or bimetallic lugs and pads where aluminum meets copper,
- correct bolt torque and spring (Belleville) washers,
- oxide-inhibiting compound where specified.

Inside a quality transformer these joints are welded or crimped under controlled conditions. External terminals are normally copper or plated, so site crews make standard connections.

## Which should you specify?

**Choose copper when:**

- space or weight in an existing vault or e-house is tight,
- the specification or owner standard requires it,
- you want maximum short-circuit margin in a compact unit.

**Choose aluminum when:**

- first cost matters and space is available,
- the unit is a standard distribution or dry-type transformer,
- you want to reduce exposure to copper price swings.

**Allow either when** you write the specification around guaranteed losses, impedance, temperature rise and sound level. This invites the most competitive bids.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is copper better than aluminum for transformer windings?

Copper conducts better and is stronger, so it allows a smaller transformer. Aluminum is lighter and cheaper. A transformer designed to the same losses and temperature rise performs the same with either metal.

### Do aluminum transformers run hotter?

No. Temperature rise is a design guarantee, typically 65 °C for liquid-filled units, and both metals are designed to meet it.

### Why are most dry-type transformers aluminum-wound?

Aluminum foil LV windings are economical, handle short-circuit forces well and keep the unit lighter, which suits indoor dry-type installations.

### Can I connect copper cables to an aluminum-wound transformer?

Yes. External terminals are normally copper or tin-plated, so standard copper or aluminum lugs can be used with correct torque.
