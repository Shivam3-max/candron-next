**Transformers hum because their steel core changes shape very slightly every time it magnetizes, twice per cycle.** On a 60 Hz system that produces a tone at 120 Hz and its harmonics. The sound level depends mostly on the core design, the size of the unit and how it is installed. In offices, condos, hospitals and residential neighbourhoods, it is worth specifying and planning for.

## Where the hum comes from

- **Magnetostriction**: the core steel expands and contracts microscopically as the magnetic field rises and falls. This is the main source of noise.
- **Load noise**: magnetic forces between windings at higher loads, more noticeable on larger units.
- **Cooling fans and pumps**: broadband noise when fans run.
- **Loose parts**: panels, clamps or accessories that vibrate add rattles.

## How sound is rated

Sound levels are expressed in **A-weighted decibels, dB(A)**, measured at a set distance around the unit under the procedures of IEEE C57.12.90 or C57.12.91 (dry-type). **NEMA TR 1** lists standard maximum sound levels by type and kVA. Larger transformers are allowed higher levels.

As rough guidance, small liquid-filled distribution units are typically in the low to mid 50s dB(A), while larger dry-type units can reach the 60s dB(A). Always check the specific TR 1 table value and the manufacturer's guaranteed level for the rating you are buying.

## Decibels in practice

- **+3 dB** doubles the sound energy, and is just noticeable.
- **+10 dB** sounds roughly twice as loud.
- Outdoors, sound falls about **6 dB each time the distance doubles**.
- Indoors, hard walls reflect sound and can add several dB.

## What affects transformer noise

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| Core flux density | Higher flux means more noise |
| Core joints | Step-lap joints are quieter than mitred or butt joints |
| Core clamping | Loose clamping increases hum and vibration |
| Size (kVA) | Larger units are louder |
| Dry-type vs liquid-filled | Liquid-filled units are usually quieter; fluid and tank damp sound |
| Harmonics | Distorted current adds higher-frequency tones |
| Mounting | Rigid mounting transmits vibration into the building |

## How to reduce noise

**At the design stage**

- Specify a **guaranteed sound level** below the standard TR 1 value, for example "TR 1 minus 5 dB".
- Lower flux density and step-lap cores reduce noise at source. See [core construction](/blog/transformer-core-construction).
- Consider a liquid-filled or cast-coil design where noise is critical.

**At installation**

- Use the **vibration isolation pads** supplied, and do not short-circuit them with rigid conduit.
- Connect with **flexible conduit or bus connections**.
- Keep transformers away from corners and hard reflective walls, and off lightweight floors over occupied spaces.
- Locate outdoor units away from property lines, windows and air intakes, and use distance first.

**After installation**

- Tighten loose enclosure panels.
- Consider sound enclosures or barrier walls for persistent issues.
- Check for harmonics if the tone sounds harsh or high-pitched.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why do transformers hum?

The core steel changes shape slightly each time it magnetizes, a process called magnetostriction. It happens twice per cycle, producing a 120 Hz hum on a 60 Hz system.

### How loud is a transformer?

It depends on size and type. Small distribution transformers are often in the 50s dB(A); large dry-type units can reach the 60s dB(A). NEMA TR 1 lists standard maximum levels.

### Is a louder transformer a sign of a problem?

A change in sound can be. New rattles, buzzing or a harsher tone may point to loose parts, overloading, overvoltage or harmonics, and should be checked.

### Can I specify a quieter transformer?

Yes. Specify a guaranteed sound level below the NEMA TR 1 standard, and the designer will reduce flux density or adjust core construction to meet it.
