**A transformer core is a stack of thin, insulated electrical-steel sheets that carries the magnetic field linking the windings.** How that steel is chosen, cut and stacked decides the transformer's no-load losses, its noise and its excitation current. Because the core is energized every hour of every day, even small improvements in core quality pay back for decades.

## Why the core is laminated

A solid steel block would allow large circulating **eddy currents**, which waste energy as heat. Splitting the core into thin sheets, each coated with a thin insulating layer, breaks those current paths. Typical laminations are **0.23 mm to 0.30 mm** thick; thinner sheets mean lower eddy-current loss but cost more to cut and stack.

## Grain-oriented electrical steel

Distribution and power transformers use **grain-oriented (GO) silicon steel**. During rolling, the crystals are aligned so magnetic flux flows very easily along the rolling direction. Higher grades, often called high-permeability or domain-refined steel, cut losses further.

What this means on a quotation: two transformers with the same kVA can have very different **no-load losses** because of steel grade and design flux density. Ask for guaranteed losses, not just efficiency.

## Core-type vs shell-type

- **Core-type** (most common in North America): windings surround the core legs. Three-phase units usually have three legs (or five legs for some designs).
- **Shell-type**: the core surrounds the windings. Used mainly for some large power and special-purpose units.

## Joints: butt, mitred and step-lap

Where the legs meet the top and bottom yokes, flux has to cross a joint. Joints are the weak point of any core.

- **Butt joints** are simple but leave large air gaps and raise losses.
- **Mitred joints** are cut at 45° so flux follows the grain around the corner.
- **Step-lap joints** stagger the mitred joints over several layers in small steps. This spreads the flux transfer, lowers no-load loss, cuts excitation current and reduces noise.

Step-lap construction is now standard for quality distribution and power transformers.

## Wound cores

Many single-phase and smaller three-phase distribution transformers use **wound cores**: a continuous strip of steel wound into a loop, annealed, then cut and laced back through the coils. Wound cores make efficient use of the grain direction and are economical in volume production.

## Amorphous metal cores

**Amorphous metal** has no crystal structure at all. It magnetizes with very little loss, cutting no-load losses by roughly 60–70% compared with conventional silicon steel. The trade-offs are higher cost, a larger and heavier core for the same rating, and slightly higher noise. Amorphous cores make most sense for lightly loaded units energized around the clock, such as utility distribution.

## Clamping, bracing and grounding

The stacked core is held by steel clamps, tie rods or bands. Good clamping:

- keeps the laminations tight so they do not vibrate and hum,
- holds the windings firmly in place against short-circuit forces, and
- keeps the core ground connection secure.

The core must be **grounded at exactly one point**. A second, unintended ground forms a loop that carries current and can produce hot spots and gas in the oil. A core-ground insulation test is a simple check during factory testing and commissioning.

## Flux density: the design choice you rarely see

Designers choose how hard to drive the steel, its **peak flux density**. Running the core harder makes it smaller and cheaper, but increases no-load losses, excitation current, inrush current and noise, and leaves less margin for overvoltage. A conservative flux density is a sign of a transformer designed for life-cycle value rather than lowest price.

## What to look for on a quotation

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Guaranteed no-load loss (W) | Paid every hour the unit is energized |
| Steel grade and joint type | Indicates loss and noise performance |
| Design flux density | Affects losses, inrush and overvoltage margin |
| Sound level (dB) | Driven largely by core design |
| Core ground test in FAT | Confirms a single, intact ground |

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a transformer core made of?

Thin sheets of grain-oriented silicon electrical steel, each insulated by a thin surface coating, stacked or wound into a closed magnetic path. Some energy-efficient units use amorphous metal instead.

### What is a step-lap core?

A core in which the joints between legs and yokes are staggered in several small steps across the lamination layers. It lowers no-load losses, excitation current and noise compared with simple mitred or butt joints.

### Does core design affect transformer noise?

Yes. Transformer hum comes mainly from magnetostriction, the tiny change in steel size as it magnetizes. Flux density, joint design and clamping all influence the sound level.
